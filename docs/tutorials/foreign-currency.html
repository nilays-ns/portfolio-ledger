Foreign currency investor

An NRI or global investor with assets in USD, EUR, or GBP alongside Indian holdings. The calculator is currency-agnostic — here's how to use it across currencies.

Who this is for

You earn in a foreign currency (or hold foreign assets) — perhaps you're an NRI with a US brokerage account and NRE deposits back home, or a resident Indian investing in international ETFs via Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS). Your portfolio spans two currencies and you want a unified view.

Portfolio Ledger has no built-in currency conversion — but this is a feature, not a limitation. You choose how to handle it.

Two approaches to multi-currency portfolios

Approach A: Single currency, converted values

Convert everything to one currency (e.g., INR) at today's exchange rate. Enter all values in INR. For USD assets, adjust the return rate to account for expected INR/USD depreciation: historically the rupee has depreciated ~2–3% per year against the dollar. So a US equity fund returning 10% USD CAGR might be entered as 12–13% in INR terms.

This is the simpler approach and works well for planning purposes.

Approach B: Separate currency portfolios

Build two separate plans — one in INR, one in USD — and keep them as separate exported files. Review them independently. This avoids mixing inflation rates (US inflation ~3% vs. India inflation ~6%) and keeps exchange rate assumptions explicit.

Step-by-step (Approach A — NRI example)

  1. Enter all assets with INR values

    Convert your USD holdings to INR at today's exchange rate. The values will be slightly stale as the rate moves, but for long-term planning this is fine — recalibrate once a year.

  2. Set USD asset return rates in INR terms

    For the S&P 500 ETF: US equities have historically returned ~10% USD CAGR. Add ~3% for INR depreciation → enter 13%. For NRE FD: the interest rate is already in INR terms (tax-free for NRIs), so enter the rate directly (~7–7.5%).

  3. Set your monthly contribution in INR

    If you contribute $800/month to your US brokerage and ₹20,000/month to Indian accounts, that's approximately ₹67,200 + ₹20,000 = ₹87,200/month total. Use ₹87,000 as a round number.

  4. Set milestones to 5, 10, 15

    15 years is your retirement target. The 5- and 10-year checkpoints help you see if you're on track.

  5. Use India's inflation rate (6%) for the real-terms toggle

    Since you plan to retire in India, Indian inflation is the right deflator. The calculator's inflation slider is set at 6% by default — leave it there.

What to watch for

The currency depreciation assumption embedded in the USD return rate is the biggest source of uncertainty. If the rupee depreciates faster (say, 4%/year) your INR-denominated returns from USD assets will be higher. If it slows or reverses, they'll be lower.

For a US-based investor who plans to retire in the US (spending in USD), use Approach B — build the USD portfolio separately using USD values, a ~3% US inflation rate, and S&P 500's historical ~10% USD return without any depreciation adjustment.
