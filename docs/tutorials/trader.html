Active trader

High-conviction bets in stocks and crypto alongside a core portfolio. The calculator can model aggressive return assumptions — but this walkthrough is also about understanding what those assumptions really mean.

Who this is for

You actively trade individual stocks, hold some crypto, and maintain a core SIP as a "boring" foundation. Your trading portfolio has had strong recent returns and you want to model where it might go — while being honest about the risk.

This walkthrough models a mixed portfolio and shows how to use the return rate as a tool for scenario analysis rather than a prediction.

Step-by-step

  1. Enter the portfolio and set monthly investment to ₹30,000

    Set flat period to 0 (no flat period — step up from year 1). Step up every 3 years. Step-up amount: 20%.

  2. Set milestones to 5, 10, 20

    Look at the 20-year projection. With 18% on stocks and 25% on crypto, the numbers will look extraordinary. Write them down.

  3. Run the pessimistic scenario

    Change stocks to 8% (a bad run — roughly flat in real terms) and crypto to 0% (no growth). Observe how dramatically the 20-year total changes. This is the downside scenario.

  4. Run the realistic scenario

    Set stocks to 14% (achievable with a good stock-picker over 10+ years) and crypto to 15% (conservative for a long-held BTC position). This is your middle-case scenario.

  5. Compare all three — optimistic, realistic, pessimistic

    Export each scenario with a descriptive filename. The spread between your optimistic and pessimistic scenarios is your risk. If the pessimistic 20-year outcome still meets your retirement goal, you can afford the risk. If it doesn't, reduce the allocation to high-risk assets.

What the numbers mean

The most important takeaway from this exercise is how sensitive high-return assumptions are. A 25% crypto return vs. a 5% crypto return over 20 years is the difference between a transformative outcome and a rounding error — and both are plausible.

The calculator models a constant return rate. In reality, high-volatility assets like individual stocks and crypto experience large drawdowns. A 50% drawdown followed by a 100% recovery still results in a 0% net return over that period, even though the mathematical average of −50% and +100% is +25%. This is called volatility drag — and it means the realistic CAGR of a volatile asset is always lower than the average of its annual returns.

A note on crypto: Historical returns for Bitcoin and Ethereum are extraordinary over their short existence. There is no long historical record to base a 20-year CAGR assumption on. Use a wide scenario range (0% to 20%) rather than a single number, and size the position such that the 0% scenario doesn't derail your retirement plan.
